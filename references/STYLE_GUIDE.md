# STYLE GUIDE — Unified decorative series with light likeness

> **Series style first. Pet hints second.**

The target is a stylized decorative pet series with roughly 45%–60% resemblance. Viewers should notice one shared visual system before noticing differences between pets.

## 1. Fixed series grammar

Keep stable across every pet:

- square card, single pet, flat paper-color background;
- pet occupies about 52%–70% with informal unequal margins, adjusted for pose and lettering;
- blunt black outer contour 2–3 times heavier than interior marks;
- simple irregular round/dot eyes, large graphic nose and weak or absent mouth;
- slightly oversized head, source-informed pose family and chunky uneven paws;
- marker/crayon fill with gaps, overlaps and uneven pressure;
- contrast-aware handwritten English around the pet plus one small accent family.

Consistency should come from repeated visual logic, not copying the exact same dog template.

## 2. Light-Likeness Card

Choose only 2–4 source hints:

1. coat color or one major marking family;
2. ear family;
3. broad face or body tendency;
4. optionally one memorable cue: beard, brow, blaze or mismatched ear.

Do not preserve exact eye/nose coordinates, individual gaze, detailed muzzle geometry, full marking map, coat direction, real stance or weight distribution.

## 3. Face

- use small flat black dots, beans or simple ovals;
- allow mild eye-spacing variation, but neutralize the source's complex expression;
- use one large flat oval or rounded-triangle nose;
- reduce the mouth to a tiny curved mark or omit it;
- let ear family and broad face tendency carry most differentiation.

Every face should belong to the same series, but round/long/square tendencies and ear placement may differ.

## 4. Body

- first classify the broad pose: sitting, standing, lying, running/leaping, curled/sleeping, peeking/close-up or another clear gesture;
- preserve body direction and one defining cue such as a lifted paw, stretched torso, tucked legs, curled tail or turned head;
- construct the pose from one or two broad fluffy masses and simplified chunky paws;
- ignore exact perspective, anatomy, joints and weight mechanics;
- vary only long/short, round/slim, fluffy/smoother and ear scale;
- avoid exact anatomical tracing, invented action and one universal sitting potato body.

The pose family is structural and does not count against the 2–4 appearance hints. A running photo must remain recognizably running; a lying photo must remain lying; a still photo must not be made to jump.

## 5. Line and color

- outer contour: bold, blunt, uneven and visibly handmade;
- inner marks: sparse and thinner, used for a few coat breaks or joints;
- allow minor repairs, overshoots and open joins;
- use flat marker/crayon blocks with visible paper holes;
- never use lighting, gradient, cast shadow or realistic volume.

### Controlled Messiness Budget

Increase disorder only about 15%–20% beyond the clean series base:

- 5–8 local contour repairs, doubled turns or restarted strokes;
- 2–4 small open joins and overshoots;
- 3–6 skipped, uneven or slightly out-of-register color patches;
- a few short stray construction marks around ears, beard, shoulders or paws;
- unequal accent strokes and imperfect circle/heart closures.

Keep the face, silhouette and accent hierarchy readable. The target is a casually corrected adult marker drawing, not chaotic scribbling or preschool mess.

## 6. Background and accents

Use a controlled rotating palette: cobalt blue, turquoise, mint, coral pink, butter yellow, warm cream, dusty lilac or pale sky blue. Keep one flat background per card. Honor a user-requested color when it preserves legibility; otherwise choose freely for coat contrast:

- white or cream coat: cobalt, turquoise, coral or stronger mint;
- apricot, tan or orange coat: cobalt, turquoise, mint or dusty lilac;
- black, cocoa or other dark coat: butter yellow, warm cream, mint, dusty pink or pale sky blue;
- multicolor coat: a hue that is not already dominant in the coat.

Across a set, rotate colors and avoid using the same background more than twice in a row. “Free color” still means one flat paper color with light grain—never a gradient, photographed floor, room scene or elaborate texture.

## 7. Text-rich surrounding layout

Treat lettering as part of the illustration rather than a caption. Fill roughly 25%–40% of the background around the pet:

- one large, crooked 1–2 word headline near the top or upper-left;
- 1–2 short stacked phrases along the left side;
- 1–2 short stacked phrases along the right side;
- optionally one small footer phrase along the bottom edge.

Use 3–5 phrases and roughly 12–22 words total. Vary letter size, tilt, baseline and spacing. Keep the handwriting rough and marker-like, never typeset or calligraphic. Exact user wording overrides automatic copy. Otherwise use short, stable phrases such as `HAPPY`, `GOOD DOGGY`, `ONLY YOU`, `SO FLUFFY`, `LOVE YOU`, `CUTEST`, `WITH YOU I'M NOT AFRAID`, `YOU ARE MY LITTLE HAPPINESS`, or `MY BEST GIFT`.

Choose text color from background value:

- dark background: paper white, warm cream or butter yellow;
- light background: charcoal black, deep navy or dark desaturated blue;
- medium background: whichever produces the strongest visible contrast.

One muted accent color may highlight one word. All other words should use the primary contrast color. Do not copy fixed slogans from reference artwork. Reject misspelled or garbled text; retry with fewer phrases when needed.

Keep a clear irregular gutter around the dog and never cross its face or silhouette. The sides should feel intentionally filled, but the text must remain subordinate to the pet.

For diagonal running or jumping poses, place lettering in the open wedges above, behind and ahead of the motion rather than forcing left/right columns. For lying or curled poses, use the upper half and corner spaces. Let text placement respond to the pose silhouette.

## 8. Output modes

- **Text-rich:** 3–5 phrases plus 2–5 graphic marks.
- **No-text:** no letters of any kind; use 4–8 pet-responsive graphic marks.
- **Paired:** create the no-text pose master first, then add lettering to a derived version while preserving dog, action, palette and line language.

## 9. Pet-responsive accent marks

With text present, use only 2–5 surrounding graphic marks. Derive the primary line family from one visible pet cue:

- floppy or folded ears: loose curved ripples beside the ears;
- upright ears: short radiating ticks above or beside the ears;
- beard or long muzzle: broken waves or zigzags near the cheeks;
- round fluffy coat: soft cloud loops or scalloped strokes;
- long or low body: elongated horizontal swashes;
- spots or patches: a small cluster of irregular dots or broken blobs;
- mismatched ears: intentionally different marks on the left and right;
- bandana or collar: 2–3 rough diagonal ticks or tiny triangle echoes;
- curled tail: one loose spiral or wave.

Add at most one secondary family such as a heart, speech bubble or star. Marks stay asymmetrical, use the same rough tool language, and sit around rather than across the pet. Lettering fills the large spaces; symbols only close small visual gaps.

## 10. Balance test

At thumbnail size, viewers should first see the shared outline/face/body system, then notice 2–4 hints. If the exact animal's gaze and facial geometry dominate, likeness is too high. If swapping coat color leaves an interchangeable puppy, likeness is too low.
